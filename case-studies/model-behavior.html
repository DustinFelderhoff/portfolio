Shaping How an AI Agent Behaves, End to End

Every AI product is a string of decisions nobody writes down: what the agent says, which model powers it, how you know it got better. I have spent the last year making those decisions by hand, in production, for a product real families use.

The Part Nobody Sees

Story Sundays is the product people meet. It records family stories so my son hears his grandmother's voice years from now. That's the reason it exists. But underneath it there is a second product, and it's the one I want to talk about here.

Underneath is an AI system with opinions. It writes prompts personalized to each family member so a grandparent knows what to say. It transcribes, summarizes, and categorizes recordings. It runs an AI family assistant that answers with context about who is in the family and what was already recorded. Every one of those features is a place where I decided what the model should do, which model should do it, and how I would know it did it well.

The point I'm making: I did not just ship AI features. I owned the behavior of an agent, the models behind it, and the evals that kept it honest. That's the whole job, done at small scale on purpose, where a wrong call costs real families their trust.

Model Choice Was the Real Product Decision

Most people pick one model and stop. I kept changing the stack on purpose, and I kept notes on why. The pipeline that builds Story Sundays has been through three architectures in four months, and each change came from a measurable pain, not novelty.

Early 2026
An eight-agent Claude Code pipeline with a CrewAI model map. Powerful, but it burned cost fast. I canceled the Claude subscription in July because the price did not earn the behavior.
July 2026
A GLM-5.2 plus DeepSeek Flash stack. One strong reasoning model with a cheap deterministic worker. Better cost, but I wanted sharper role separation between thinking and verifying.
July 2026 to now
DeepSeek Pro for architecture, planning, and complex reasoning. DeepSeek Flash for the mechanical work: verification, linting, doc sync. A five-role cycler where each role runs in a fresh context.

The lesson I carry from this: a model is not a brand. It's a cost and behavior tradeoff you make per task. You want the cheap, deterministic model doing the checks you can define, and the strong model doing the thinking you can't. When a tool stops earning its place, you cut it. That reasoning, not any single vendor, is the skill.

Evals Are How I Steer

An AI product gets better only if you can tell it got better. Story Sundays runs tests at four layers, and each layer may only claim what it can actually observe.

Unit

Pure logic and rendering against mocks. Fast, but it proves only what the mock allows.

Hook-integration

Real local Postgres, no mocks. Proves a data function reads and writes real rows.

Integration

Real Postgres with row-level security. Proves policies and foreign keys hold.

End to end

A real browser driving the full click-to-database flow the way a user meets it.

The rule I operate on is written at the top of the project: a green test suite is not proof a bug is fixed. A test that mocks the very thing it claims to verify passes no matter what. So I certify at the lowest layer that can see the behavior, and I keep about 1,900 lines of edge-case rules that a change may never break.

Tools I Live Inside, and Why

The job is not picking a tool and using it. It's knowing why each tool earns its spot. Here is my daily stack and the reasoning behind it.

  • Hermes is my orchestrator. It routes work to specialized sub-agents so each step runs in a fresh context, which stops one agent from writing a test that passes by construction.
  • opencode and Open Code Go run the build work. I route by task: a cheap, fast model for deterministic chores, a stronger one for architecture. I test new models as they ship and keep the ones that earn it.
  • Claude Code and Codex come and go. I used Claude Code heavily, then cut it when cost outran behavior. The tools change; the habit of re-deciding them doesn't.
  • A humanizer gate runs on everything I publish, because AI output has a tell, and I refuse to ship writing that reads like a committee wrote it.

Those releases moved through a gated pipeline with circuit breakers. When a change threatened a core invariant, the gate stopped it before it reached users. Speed with a stopping condition is not the same as speed. One of them is shipping; the other is chaos with a budget.

The failure that made it better

I pushed the AI audit tool to production before training the team that handled its exceptions. Within two weeks they were flagging everything, because they did not trust it. The system was technically right and operationally broken. I pulled it back, ran three hands-on sessions, and rebuilt the exception flow with their input. The second launch stuck. The same failure taught me about the pipeline itself: agents can write tests that prove nothing. Trust, whether from a person or a test, has to be earned at the layer that can actually see the truth.

What I Would Bring to a Model Behavior Team

A team that owns how an agent behaves spends its days exactly where I already work: deciding what behavior is right, picking the path to it, and proving it with evals before it ships. That is not a stretch assignment for me. It's the shape of the work I have done every week for the last year, on a product where a mistake cost people the trust of their own families.

Bring me in and I will be in the prompts, the code, and the data. I will ship every week and I will show my work with evals, not vibes. And when a model stops earning its place, I will be the one who says so.
